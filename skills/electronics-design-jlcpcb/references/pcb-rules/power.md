# PCB rules: power circuits

Purpose: placement and routing rules for switching converters, linear regulators, input filters, eFuses and load switches, capacitors, power distribution, branch protection, crowbars, batteries and chargers, and inductive or switched loads.

The method (worksheet, returns, fill, vias, rule words) is in `general.md`. The part's own layout section and its evaluation board come first: copy their rules into `LAYOUT_RULES_LEDGER.md` with the figure number, then apply the rules below to whatever the datasheet leaves open.

## 1. Placement priority

Place in this order. Never move an earlier item far away to shorten a ratsnest elsewhere.
1. Each converter's hot loop: the parts its topology puts in the loop (section 2, PWR-01), packed at the switch pins and power ground as the maker's layout figure shows.
2. The rest of the power stage: the inductor or transformer at the switch node, the other input and output capacitors, and the local bypass capacitors the datasheet asks for at the chip's supply pins.
3. Bootstrap capacitor, snubber and boot-resistor footprints, feedback divider, compensation.
4. eFuse or load-switch bypass, output clamp, and its setting parts at their pins.
5. Protection at the input connector (`protection-esd.md`).
6. The crowbar or clamp at the rail it protects.
7. Quiet clusters (references, timers, reset and supervisor circuits) away from items 1 to 6.
8. Branch protection and filters at their cable exits.
9. Connectors and test points, inside the frozen mechanical positions.

Why: the parasitics of the first items decide whether the circuit works; the rest can stretch.

## 2. Switching converters

**Hot loop**

The hot loop is where the current jumps at each switching edge. Find it from the topology: draw the current path in each switch state; the branches whose current changes abruptly between the states form the loop, and the node whose voltage jumps is the switch node. Starting points by topology (the maker's layout figure for the exact part decides):

| Topology | Hot loop (pulsed current) | Smooth current, less critical |
|---|---|---|
| Buck | Input ceramic, high-side switch, low-side switch or diode | Inductor and output capacitors |
| Boost | Output ceramic, rectifier (diode or synchronous switch), low-side switch, back through power ground (TI SNVA731 section 6) | Inductor and input capacitors |
| Four-switch buck-boost | Two loops: input ceramic with the input switch pair, output ceramic with the output switch pair; in buck-boost operation both carry pulsed current | The inductor |
| Flyback | Primary: input ceramic, primary winding, switch, sense resistor. Secondary: secondary winding, rectifier, output ceramic. And the clamp or snubber loop across the primary or the switch | The bulk capacitors behind the loop ceramics |
| Any other (SEPIC, Cuk, inverting, multi-phase, resonant) | From the switch-state paths, and the maker's layout procedure for that part | |

- PWR-01 MUST name each converter's hot loop or loops from the table and the maker's figure, and close each with at least one small ceramic placed directly across it, on the same layer as the switches, with no via in the loop (a buck's input ceramic, a boost's output ceramic, both of a four-switch buck-boost). Local bypass capacitors the datasheet asks for at other pins (the supply pin of a boost controller, VCC, the bias supply) are placed as it asks, but they are not the hot loop. Why: the loop carries the fastest current edges; its inductance sets switch-node ringing and emissions, and the buck habit of putting the input capacitor first enlarges a boost's real loop.
- PWR-02 MUST NOT let a bulk or electrolytic capacitor stand in for that ceramic. Rank the loop's capacitors by effective capacitance and ESL at the operating voltage, not by printed value. Why: a bulk part's ESL is far too high for the switching edges.
- PWR-03 MUST close the hot loop on the converter's own layer (the loop ceramic's ground pad straight to the PGND pin or the low-side switch) and keep the plane of the next layer (layer 2 of a 4-layer board) solid directly below, so the image current lowers the loop inductance. Why: a via in the loop adds inductance (PWR-01); the plane below shrinks the loop's field, but it cannot rescue long topside connections between parts.
- PWR-04 SHOULD keep a hot-loop table per loop ceramic: which loop it closes, which end faces the ground pin, and the path that closes the loop. Why: a reviewer can accept or reject each orientation on evidence.
- PWR-05 MUST use 4 layers with the layer plan of `general.md` GEN-03 for a board with a switching converter (phase 8): a ground plane right next to the parts is the better-performing choice for the hot loop, the switch node and emissions. 2 layers are accepted only when all three hold: the converter's own maker shows a 2-layer layout with its measured results (an evaluation board on 2 layers); that layout is copied (hot loop, ground, keep-outs); and no receiver, antenna or sensitive analog input shares the board. Write which case applies in the design note. In every other case 4 layers; a 2-layer board outside these conditions is a FAIL unless the user waives it in writing (section 2 of `general.md`). Why: a plane next to the parts shortens the hot loop and shields the switch node, which is why makers recommend it; a 2-layer converter layout is trusted only where its maker has measured it, and the step to 4 layers costs little next to a re-layout.

**Switch node and inductor**
- PWR-06 MUST give each switch node only the connections the circuit needs (the switch pins, the inductor or winding pad, the rectifier or the other switch, the bootstrap capacitor, the snubber pad): short, wide enough for each branch's RMS current, with the smallest copper area. Measure and record the area. Why: switch-node copper is a capacitive antenna; a big polygon "for current" makes it worse.
- PWR-07 MUST NOT route feedback, references, timing, reset, buses, CC or any cable signal under or beside the switch node, bootstrap copper or inductor, on any layer. Put a signal keep-out on the other layers under the switch copper and the inductor courtyard, and a copper keep-out between the inductor pads on the top layer. The ground plane stays solid under them unless the maker's layout cuts it (a ledger entry). Why: capacitive and magnetic coupling; the solid plane is the shield and keeps the loop small.
- PWR-08 MUST check the inductor with the topology's own equations, at the lowest inductance tolerance: saturation current at the hottest temperature at or above the converter's maximum switch-current limit (or at or above the peak inductor current I_Lpk plus margin, where the maker allows that), and RMS heating at the worst input voltage. Buck: I_Lpk = I_out + dI/2, dI = (Vin - Vout) * D / (L * f), D = Vout / Vin, worst at the highest input. Boost: I_Lpk = I_out / (1 - D) + dI/2, dI = Vin * D / (L * f), D = 1 - Vin * eta / Vout, worst at the lowest input. Why: start-up into large capacitance and an output short drive the inductor to the current limit, not to the load current, and a saturating inductor makes current spikes the layout cannot absorb.
- PWR-09 SHOULD place and turn a shielded inductor so its leakage field points away from sensitive circuits and cable exits; take the field direction from the maker if given. Why: the leakage field couples into nearby loops and cables.

**Operating limits and loop**
- PWR-52 MUST check each switching converter's limits over the whole input range: the on-time at the highest input (buck: t_on = Vout / (Vin_max * f); other topologies by their own duty equation) stays above the datasheet's minimum on-time with margin, and the duty at the lowest input stays below the maximum duty; the highest input including transients (the hot-plug ring, a surge, as left by the input clamp) stays below the converter's absolute maximum. Why: below the minimum on-time the converter skips pulses or loses regulation, above the maximum duty it drops out, and the clamped transient, not the nominal input, decides whether it survives.
- PWR-53 MUST, for a converter with external compensation, compute the network by the maker's method from the chosen inductor and the output capacitance at DC bias with its ESR, and confirm the phase margin with the maker's tool or a simulation (`references/simulation.md`); for a constant-on-time or hysteretic part with ceramic-only output capacitors, fit the ripple-injection network the maker shows where its control scheme needs it; and check each output capacitor's ripple-current rating at the switching frequency against the topology's RMS ripple current (buck: dI / sqrt(12); boost: about I_out * sqrt(D / (1 - D))). Why: a missing or wrong compensation or injection network makes the loop unstable or jittery, and a capacitor above its ripple rating heats and ages.
- PWR-66 MUST, for a boost, treat the input-to-output path as always closed unless the datasheet says the part disconnects it: through the inductor and the rectifier (a diode, or a synchronous switch's body diode) the input feeds the output even with the regulator off. Turning the regulator off does not clear an output short, and the input charges the output capacitors at plug-in through the inductor, limited by nothing but the circuit (peak about V_in x sqrt(C_out / L)). Where the output can be shorted (it leaves the board, or the fault table names it) or that inrush harms the source, put the short and the inrush in the fault table with the source's current, and fit an input disconnect (load switch, eFuse) or choose a boost with true output disconnect. Why: the regulator can at most turn its switch on, which diverts the current to ground but does not stop it (TI SNVA731 sections 5.1 to 5.3).

**Bootstrap, snubber and tuning**
- PWR-10 MUST place the bootstrap capacitor at the BOOT and SW pins with the shortest loop. Why: that loop carries the gate-drive current edges.
- PWR-11 SHOULD add a footprint for a bootstrap series resistor (fitted at 0 ohm) and an RC snubber from the switch node to ground, unfitted by default. Put the snubber's switch-side pad on the switch copper itself, with its own ground via. Why: these are the cheap emission fixes at bring-up; a snubber with a long loop does nothing.

**Feedback and sense**
- PWR-12 MUST place the feedback divider and compensation at the FB pin, with the divider's ground on the controller's quiet ground. Why: the divider's ground is the regulator's reference.
- PWR-13 MUST take the sense from the regulated node at the output capacitors (a buck: after the inductor; a boost: at the output capacitor, never between the rectifier and the capacitor, TI SNVA731 section 6) on its own track: never from the switch side, never from a loaded branch track. Use remote sense at the load only when the maker allows it and the loop is designed for it. Why: sensing on the switch side or a loaded track regulates the wrong voltage.
- PWR-14 MUST route the feedback track short and away from the switch node, bootstrap and inductor, and record its distance to them. Why: FB is a high-impedance node; injected noise becomes output jitter.
- PWR-15 SHOULD put a loop-injection resistor of 10-51 ohm in the top of the divider, fitted from the start and reachable with probes. Its DC error is the divider current times R (about 2.5 mV at 50 uA and 51 ohm): small, but count it in the set-point tolerance. Why: it allows a Bode measurement on the bench without rework; a 0 ohm part would have to be replaced first.
- PWR-16 MUST keep the power-good line away from the switch node and never parallel to it. Why: coupled spikes make power-good report a fault that is not there.

**Analog and power ground**
- PWR-17 MUST follow the datasheet's ground rule. Where it asks for one point between analog and power ground: make an analog ground island (its own net through a net-tie footprint, or an island of the ground plane cut by a ring) joined at one bridge near the power-ground pin. Keep every analog via and pad wholly inside the island; no stitching via in it; no track on an adjacent layer crosses the ring's gap. Why: a second tie lets power current flow through the quiet reference, and a track over the gap loses its return path there.
- PWR-18 MUST prove the single point by the method of its variant. Net-tie: set shorts between nets as DRC errors and check that only the net-tie footprint joins analog-ground copper to ground copper. Same-net island: a cut test with `scripts/zone_check.py` (`[[zone.single_point]]`), or by hand: in a copy, close the bridge (for example with a no-copper rule area), refill and run `scripts/drc_run.py`; the analog copper must come out unconnected. Run either check once on a correct and once on a broken copy, so you know it can both pass and fail. Why: one board had three hidden ties, a first version of the test failed every board because the checker was wrong, and with a net-tie the cut test never reports "unconnected".
- PWR-19 MUST NOT split the main ground plane into large separate areas to build a "star". The single point is local to the controller. Why: a board-wide star makes long, narrow high-current returns.

**Heat**
- PWR-20 MUST provide the thermal vias and copper the datasheet asks for, inside the exposed pad as the exception of `dfm-dft.md` DFM-40 allows, landing in copper of the exposed pad's own net (per the datasheet pin table), much larger than the pad, on the far layer or an inner plane; record that area beside the via count, record any shortfall, and put a thermal measurement on the bench list. Why: the part's thermal ratings assume the maker's copper, and the receiving copper does most of the cooling; vias into a small island give little, and some exposed pads are VIN, SW or drain, or must float, so a via into the ground plane would short them.

## 3. Linear regulators

- PWR-21 MUST compute dissipation at the highest input voltage, P = (Vin_max - Vout) * I_load + Vin_max * I_q, and the junction temperature from the local ambient and a thermal resistance for the copper you actually give it (`thermal.md` THM-05 says where that figure comes from). Why: the datasheet's junction-to-ambient figure is for a test board, not yours.
- PWR-22 MUST give the tab or exposed pad the copper and vias the maker shows, and keep heat-sensitive parts (references, PTCs, electrolytics, sensors) away from it. Why: the copper is the heat sink, and its heat drifts or derates the neighbours.
- PWR-23 MUST place input and output capacitors at the pins and check the output capacitor's ESR against the regulator's stability range. Why: some regulators oscillate with low-ESR ceramics and need a small series resistor.
- PWR-54 MUST check dropout and supply rejection. Dropout: the lowest input, minus the drop ahead of the regulator at full load (protection, filter, copper), minus the maximum dropout at the load current and the hottest junction temperature, stays at or above the output set-point at its upper tolerance; where the design accepts dropout on purpose (a battery near empty), the requirement says so and the output in dropout still meets the load's minimum. Supply rejection, where the regulator follows a switching converter: read it from the datasheet curve at the switching frequency, the real headroom and the load current; input ripple * 10^(-PSRR / 20), with PSRR in dB as a positive number, stays within the rail's allowed ripple. Why: near dropout a linear regulator loses regulation and most of its rejection, and a converter's ripple at its switching frequency can pass with little attenuation.

## 4. Input filters and damping

- PWR-24 MUST place the input filter (inductor or ferrite, damping capacitor, converter input ceramics) as one compact block next to the converter, and keep switching current behind it. Why: long traces between filter parts change the filter and let switching current reach the input cable.
- PWR-25 MUST check damping against the converter's negative input resistance, R_in = -Vin_min^2 / P_in_max: the filter's output impedance peak stays well below |R_in| at every corner (source impedance, ESR, DC-biased capacitance, minimum input voltage, maximum power). Record the margin in dB. Why: an undamped filter and a converter form an oscillator.
- PWR-26 MUST use datasheet ESR and DC-biased capacitance, not schematic values. Why: a ceramic substitution can make a stable filter resonant.
- PWR-27 MUST back-annotate long power-path parasitics from the layout (resistance, inductance and capacitance of a long pour) into the stability and fault simulations. Why: a long pour adds milliohms and nanohenries that change damping and fault peaks.

## 5. eFuses, hot-swap controllers and load switches

- PWR-28 MUST make IN and OUT paths short and wide, with the top and bottom copper the maker asks for heat spreading. Where a maker asks for paths sized for twice the full-load current, apply it to the normal and fault-blanking current of this part, not blindly to every cable. Why: the part's own losses and fault pulses need copper to spread their heat.
- PWR-29 MUST place the input bypass at IN, and the output clamp diode and capacitor at OUT and ground. Why: on a fast trip the downstream inductance pulls OUT below ground; only a clamp at the pins absorbs it.
- PWR-30 MUST place the current-limit, slew-rate, timer and under- and over-voltage parts at their pins on a quiet ground, away from switching edges. No test-point stub on the current-limit node: probe on the component pad. Why: these nodes set limits and timing; noise there trips the part or moves its limit.
- PWR-31 MUST list the package's own narrow pad bars as package-limited necks in the worksheet, with an infrared bench check at full load. Why: the pad cannot grow without another package, so the bench must prove it stays cool.
- PWR-32 MUST find the reverse-current path when the output is driven from outside (a powered cable, a back-feeding peripheral) and choose a blocking part, an ideal diode, or a written warning explicitly. Why: an outside source once kept a whole device on through a load switch with no reverse blocking.
- PWR-33 SHOULD simulate start-up junction temperature at every slew-rate corner (`references/simulation.md`). Why: start-up into large capacitance is the hardest moment for the part.

## 6. Capacitors

- PWR-34 MUST compute each ceramic's effective capacitance at its DC bias and temperature from the maker's curves, and meet the datasheet's requirement with the effective value. Why: a 1 uF 50 V X5R 0603 part kept only 0.35-0.5 uF at 40 % of its rated voltage.
- PWR-35 MUST check the dielectric's temperature rating against the local temperature. Why: X5R is rated to 85 C; next to a converter in a warm enclosure that may not be enough.
- PWR-36 MUST check electrolytic polarity on the footprint, ripple current at the switching frequency, and lifetime at the local temperature. Why: a reversed electrolytic fails violently, and heat shortens its life quickly.
- PWR-37 MUST place decoupling first, at its pin, ranked by mounted loop inductance, not by value: nearest the pin goes the capacitor with the lowest-inductance mounting that serves the fastest current, on copper as wide as the pad, vias at the pad, on the side next to the ground plane (estimates: about 0.4 nH per mm for a 0.25 mm track over a plane 0.2 mm below; about 0.8 nH per mm on a 1.6 mm 2-layer board). Repeat one value rather than decade steps unless a simulation shows no new peak; datasheet-mandated values and band capacitors (PWR-38) still win. Where a ceramic sits beside an electrolytic or tantalum bulk part, simulate the pair and keep the impedance peak between them under the rail's target (PWR-49). Keep a pad-to-pin table (distance, squares) for the critical ones. Why: above self-resonance the mounting sets a capacitor's impedance, mixed values add resonance peaks, and decoupling placed late ends up wherever room is left.
- PWR-38 SHOULD choose bypass capacitors at cable exits by their mounted self-resonance in the victim band (`rf.md` RF-82): the maker's ESL plus pads, track and ground via (estimate: about 1.3 nH for one through via of a 1.6 mm board, so the via can set the resonance). Check the window in which each stays below a few ohms, not only the resonance, and simulate it with the larger capacitor beside it so their parallel-resonance peak stays out of every victim band. Each has its own ground via, in line with the power pin. Why: a 1 nF part self-resonates near 200 MHz and does nothing at GHz, and a part added for one band can raise the noise in another.
- PWR-49 MUST write, per rail in the design note's rail table, the target impedance with its units, its frequency range and its observation point (the load's pins): Z_target [ohm] = dV_dyn [V] / dI [A], or V_rail [V] x f_dyn / dI [A] with f_dyn a plain fraction (5 % = 0.05). dI is the worst load step (without better data about half the maximum current); dV_dyn is what the load's voltage window leaves after the regulator's DC tolerance and the copper drop (GEN-23), so no millivolt is counted twice, and it is the bench pass limit at that point. Example: 3.3 V rail, 50 mV, 0.5 A step: 0.1 ohm. The range runs from where the regulator's loop stops answering to the highest frequency of the load's current that the board can still influence. Starting heuristics for a digital chip, with their conditions: the package usually limits the board's reach to about 100 MHz (less with high package inductance or on-package capacitors); at about 1 ohm or more the board's capacitors rarely decide, and the maker's per-pin capacitors are kept as they are; below it, at least n > 2 pi x f_max x L_mounted / Z_target capacitors (a best case: equal parts, mounted inductances only in parallel, no shared via or plane inductance, no resonance) and a rail-impedance simulation with a 1 A AC source over the range, no peak above the target. A slow load (relay, heater) or a sensitive analog rail (reference, low-noise amplifier) takes its range and limit from its own current spectrum and noise need, not from these figures. Check the load step (PWR-51) and the regulator's stability beside it. Why: it shows which rails need engineering and gives the bench a number; a ripple in millivolts entered where a fraction was meant moves the target by orders of magnitude, and the heuristics hold only where their assumptions do (Bogatin, Signal and Power Integrity - Simplified, pp. 691-695, 713-714, 717, 754-755).
- PWR-50 MUST sum per regulator the effective capacitance it drives at DC bias (its own output capacitors plus all downstream decoupling and bulk), write it in the rail table, and check it against the datasheet's maximum output capacitance and against the minimum current limit during soft-start: C_total x V_out / t_soft-start plus the start-up load stays below that limit. Otherwise lengthen the soft-start or move bulk behind a slew-controlled load switch. Include this capacitance in the start-up simulation. Why: too much capacitance trips a converter's over-current protection into hiccup at turn-on, or pushes an LDO into shutdown.
- PWR-51 MUST size the local capacitance of every load with current steps (radio bursts, LED or flash pulses, motors, relay coils, displays) as C >= dI x t / dV, with t the time until the regulator responds (from its loop bandwidth, or the pulse length if shorter), dV the load's allowed droop and C the effective value at bias (PWR-34). Place it at the load on the shortest loop, write the calculation in the design note, then simulate regulator plus capacitors. Why: until the regulator responds only the capacitors hold the rail; beyond that, more capacitance does not cut the fast spike, which loop inductance sets.

## 7. Power distribution and branch protection

- PWR-63 MUST write and prove the order of the supplies for every chip with more than one supply or a stated order, ramp speed or wait: which rail first, the largest difference allowed between rails, how long reset or enable is held, and the same for switching off and for a short dip of the input. State the mechanism (an enable chain driven by power-good, a supervisor, a delay with its tolerance), check that no pin is driven while its chip is unpowered (`mcu-digital.md`, section 7), and prove it by a start-up and shut-down simulation with every rail and its real capacitance, or by a bench test with numeric limits. Why: rails in the wrong order latch up a chip, start it in a wrong state or wear it out slowly; a rule check knows nothing about time.
- PWR-62 MUST give every regulator, linear or switching, the copper and vias its current and its heat need, with numbers (when copper is not enough, the next steps are in `thermal.md`: a path into the enclosure, a heat sink, a fan), and prove it twice: a row in the design note's heat table, for a regulator that loses more than about 0.1 W (`thermal.md` section 1; dissipation at the worst case, the copper area and vias on the board, the estimated temperature against the limit), and rows in the current worksheet for its input, its output and both returns (width, via count by area, `general.md` GEN-32). The output reaches its loads through a short, broad pour, not a signal-width track; the capacitors the datasheet asks for sit at the regulator, and further ones at loads that are far away. A regulator that both delivers and absorbs current (a bus-termination supply, a tracking supply) is sized for the larger of the two currents in both directions. Why: a regulator on a thin track with two vias passes every rule check; it then runs hot, drops voltage under load, or oscillates, and only the bench shows it.
- PWR-39 MUST lay out the power tree planned in phase 2: upstream copper carries the sum of the simultaneous downstream loads. Why: the copper must match the budget the reviews accepted.
- PWR-40 SHOULD feed secondary loads from the board, not through another load's connector or copper. Why: one connector pin once carried two loads' current past its rating.
- PWR-41 MUST place branch protection (PTC, eFuse, fuse), where the protection table gives a branch its own (a branch that leaves the board or can fault on its own), and its filter near the branch's cable exit, with the downstream capacitors on the protected side. Why: a cable fault is then cut before its current crosses the board.
- PWR-42 MUST check each PTC's hold current at the maximum LOCAL temperature against the maximum load, and its trip current against the contact and wire rating; its fault limits and tripped state by `protection-esd.md` ESD-45. Why: a PTC held only about half its rated current at 70 C.
- PWR-43 MUST keep PTCs, shunt references and other temperature-sensitive parts away from converters, inductors and hot copper (any track or pour with a worksheet rise above about 10 C): 10 mm or more worked on a proven board (starting point), or add the copper's rise to their local temperature. Write it into the layout requirements before placement. Why: heat drifts references and lowers PTC hold current, whether it comes from a regulator or a warm input pour.
- PWR-44 MUST cover partial shorts (between rated load and converter limit) in the fault table. Why: an input-side limiter sees output current divided by the conversion ratio and may never trip.

## 8. Crowbars and fault-current loops

- PWR-45 MUST make the fault loop (rail, switch, ground) short and broad on both terminals, with solid pad connections and several vias; no thermal relief, no single via. Why: the fault current is set by source impedance and stored energy, not by the normal load.
- PWR-46 MUST size copper, vias and the clamp part from the simulated fault pulse: peak current, I^2*t and energy against the part's safe operating area and avalanche energy. The normal load current does not size a crowbar. Check the copper and vias of the loop with the adiabatic pulse method of `general.md` GEN-28. Why: a crowbar is a deliberate short; its current can reach hundreds of amperes for microseconds.
- PWR-47 MUST sense the protected voltage at a quiet point and reference the comparator to ground that does not lift under the fault current. Why: ground bounce gives false trips or no trip.
- PWR-48 MUST place the clamp AT the protected rail when the energy stored between the disconnect point and that rail can damage the load on its own (the energy map of phase 2). Why: an upstream disconnect cannot stop energy already stored below it.

## 9. Batteries and chargers

- PWR-55 MUST treat a lithium cell as a safety topic: say so to the user, and follow the cell maker's and the charger maker's documents to the letter (charge voltage, charge current, temperature window). Protect the cell against over-voltage, under-voltage and over-current (a protected cell, or a protection IC with its switches) and put a fuse at the cell, ahead of everything else on the board, whose DC breaking capacity at the cell's highest voltage covers the cell's prospective short-circuit current (`protection-esd.md` ESD-44): a fuse that passes on hold current and voltage but breaks less than that current FAILS. Why: a lithium cell outside its window can vent or burn, no fuse upstream of the cell stops the energy the cell itself holds, and a cell's low internal resistance can drive more current than a small fuse can break.
- PWR-56 MUST size the battery from the energy budget of phase 2 (each operating mode with its current and its share of the time): average current = the sum of each mode's current times its time share; usable capacity = the rated capacity derated for the coldest temperature, ageing, the cut-off voltage the board really uses and the discharge rate (estimate: 70 to 80 % of the rating as a starting point); autonomy = usable capacity / average current, against the requirement. Refer every current to the cell: through a switching converter I_cell = I_rail x V_rail / (V_cell x efficiency at that load, read from the light-load curve for sleep), with V_cell the mean discharge voltage, or the end-of-discharge voltage for the peak check; through a linear regulator I_cell = I_rail plus its own current. Count everything that stays on the cell while asleep (charger, protection circuit, dividers, gauge). Give the running time twice, with typical figures at room temperature and with maximum figures at the hottest surroundings, and tell the user the lower one. Check every peak current (radio bursts, motor starts, inrush) against what the cell, its protection and its fuse can deliver at the coldest temperature and at end of life, with the drop over the cell's internal resistance leaving the regulator above its minimum input. Why: a rating read at room temperature, low current and full depth promises an autonomy the product never reaches; a boost converter from a 1.5 V cell to 3.3 V draws about 2.5 times the load current from the cell, and sleep currents rise several times when warm; a cell that cannot deliver the peak browns the board out on a full charge.
- PWR-57 MUST set the charge current from the smallest of the source's capability (the USB contract, the adapter), the cell's allowed charge rate and the charger's heat at the highest input (linear charger: P = (Vin_max - Vbat_min) * I_charge), and sense the cell temperature with the thermistor input the charger maker shows, placed at the cell. Why: a linear charger at full current from 5 V into a flat cell can dissipate watts, and charging outside the cell's temperature window damages it.
- PWR-64 MUST keep charge current out of a primary cell (a coin cell, alkaline cells): no path from another supply into it; where a diode separates them (a clock backup, a diode-OR with USB), its leakage at the hottest temperature stays below the reverse current the cell maker allows, with the series resistor the maker shows. Rechargeable lithium cells in series need protection and balancing per cell, by the protector maker's document: say in phase 1 that it is a safety topic. A cell on a plug counts as reversible: the polarity of such plugs is not standardised, so check the plug against the cell actually bought (PWR-58). Why: a primary cell that is charged can leak or burst, one cell of a series string can be overcharged while the string looks right, and a reversed cell has no current limit.
- PWR-58 MUST decide power path or load sharing (the system fed from the input while charging, or straight from the cell) so the charger's termination and safety timer see only the charge current; protect against a reversed cell where one can be fitted reversed (holders, replaceable cells, a cell on a plug: PWR-64); add a shipping mode where the product is stored with the cell connected; and block the charger's back-feed into an unpowered controller through status lines, signal pins or a rail driven from the cell (`protection-esd.md` ESD-50). Why: a load on the cell confuses termination and the timer, a reversed cell has no current limit, a stored product drains its cell, and a live charger can keep an off controller half powered.

## 10. Inductive and switched loads

- PWR-59 MUST give every relay, solenoid and motor coil an external recirculation or clamp path: a freewheel diode, or a clamp (TVS or zener) at the coil or across its switch, rated for the coil current and, for a clamp, for the energy of each turn-off (peak) and its average power (energy times the highest switching rate) at its hottest temperature. Take the energy from the real current and voltage path: about 1/2 * L * I^2 for a clamp across the coil; across the switch the supply keeps feeding the coil during the decay, about 1/2 * L * I^2 * V_clamp / (V_clamp - V_supply) (the coil's resistance lowers both). Choose the switch's voltage rating above the highest voltage it sees, with the margin of phase 3: supply + clamp voltage for a clamp across the coil (supply + a diode drop with a freewheel diode), the clamp voltage for one across the switch. A switch may clamp itself (avalanche) only where its maker rates it for repetitive avalanche, within that rating's current, starting temperature, pulse length and repetition limits (an E_AR or I_AR curve), with the average heating (avalanche plus conduction) and the peak rise per pulse in its junction budget and its safe operating area checked. A single-pulse E_AS at 25 C covers one turn-off, not repeated switching; a part with no avalanche rating must never avalanche. Why: an unclamped coil drives its switch into avalanche at every turn-off, and repeated pulses start from a junction that has not cooled (Infineon, Power MOSFET avalanche design guidelines, v1.1, eq. 1, sections 4.2 and 6).
- PWR-60 MUST size a motor's switch, copper, fuse and supply for its stall current (start-up and a blocked rotor), not its running current. Why: a motor draws its stall current at every start, and a blocked one draws it until something opens.
- PWR-65 MUST give the energy a motor returns when it brakes or reverses a place to go: the capacitance on its supply, a clamp or a braking resistor sized for it. A rail fed through a diode or a one-way regulator cannot absorb it. Compute the rise of the rail against the rating of the driver and of every part on that rail, as the driver maker's document shows. Why: a braking motor pumps its supply above the rating of the driver and of everything else on the rail.
- PWR-61 MUST give every gate or base that switches a load a resistor that holds the load off while the controller is in reset, unpowered or booting with its pins floating (pull-down for an N-channel low-side switch, pull-up to the source for a P-channel high-side switch). Why: a floating gate turns a load half on, and the switch then heats in its linear region.

## 11. Questions for each datasheet

Write each answer, with its page or figure, into `LAYOUT_RULES_LEDGER.md`:
- Which layer count and stack does the maker's layout example assume?
- Which capacitor must be closest, on which pins? Where is the hot loop in the maker's figure?
- Where does analog ground join power ground, and at how many points?
- How many thermal vias, what drill, and are they in the pad?
- How must feedback be routed, and from which node?
- Which nodes need keep-outs, on which layers? What exposed-pad paste pattern is given?
- Which inductor and capacitors did the evaluation board use, and what does its BOM add that the typical circuit omits (input resistor, boot resistor, injection resistor)?
- What maximum output capacitance, soft-start time and minimum start-up current limit does the regulator allow, and how fast does it answer a load step (loop bandwidth)?
- Switching converter: minimum on-time and maximum duty; absolute maximum input and how much room it leaves for transients; switch-current limit; internal or external compensation, and the maker's method; does the control scheme need ripple injection with ceramic output capacitors? A boost: does it disconnect the output when off (PWR-66)?
- Linear regulator: maximum dropout at our load current and hottest temperature; supply rejection at our switching frequency and headroom.
- Charger and cell: capacity against temperature, discharge rate and cut-off voltage; internal resistance and maximum discharge current; charge voltage, current and temperature window; thermistor input; termination and safety timer; power-path behaviour; shipping mode; what its pins do while the controller is off.
- Which clauses are conditional ("if the source is more than a few inches away, add bulk") and does our installation trigger them?
- What do the protection curves say at our temperature: hold current versus temperature, limit versus output voltage, safe operating area?

- Primary cell: which reverse (charging) current does the maker allow, and which series resistor does it show beside a second supply?

## Typical mistakes

- A converter maker recommended 4 layers; the board started with 2 to save a little money. The routing had to be redone on 4 layers; the 2-layer deviation was not worth defending.
- The analog ground of a converter joined the plane at three points: one via poked 0.15 mm past the island edge, one resistor pad straddled it, and one ground via sat in the cut-out and reached nothing. A cut test found it.
- The damping electrolytic sat on the source side of the input filter: 6.5 dB margin against the converter's negative resistance. Moved to the converter side: 16.5 dB.
- PTCs chosen at their 25 C rating held about half that current at the board's real temperature.
- An input-side eFuse could not see a short on the low-voltage side; the converter hiccupped forever and small ferrites overheated.
- Overvoltage protection acted upstream, while the capacitors below the cut-off could still dump into the protected rail. A crowbar at the rail was needed.
- The eFuse output lacked the maker's output clamp; a fast trip pulled the pin below its negative rating.
- The switch-node copper grew into a large polygon "for current"; a reviewer trimmed it to the pin, a short neck and the inductor pad.
- Common in the field: a board with a few hundred microfarads on its rails drew amperes of inrush from a USB source at turn-on; nobody had summed the capacitance each regulator starts into.
